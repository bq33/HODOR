# Executive Briefings

This folder contains board-ready security communications, quarterly risk summaries, and executive-level reporting. The goal is to maintain a library of materials that an AI agent can reference when drafting the next briefing, ensuring consistency in voice, format, and depth across executive communications.

## Briefing Types

**Quarterly Security Review** — Comprehensive overview of the security posture covering threat landscape changes, incident summary, detection effectiveness metrics, compliance status, and upcoming risks. Audience: C-suite and board. Format: slide deck with appendix. Template: `templates/exec-quarterly-review-template.md`.

**Incident Executive Summary** — Concise (1-2 page) summary of a significant security incident for executive awareness. Written within 48 hours of incident resolution. Covers what happened, what was affected, how it was resolved, and what is being done to prevent recurrence. Avoids technical jargon. Template: `templates/exec-incident-summary-template.md`.

**Threat Landscape Brief** — Periodic update on emerging threats relevant to Prism's sector. Highlights new threat actors, evolving attack techniques, or regulatory developments that may require executive attention or resource allocation. Template: `templates/exec-threat-brief-template.md`.

**Board Risk Dashboard** — Single-page visual summary of key security and T&S metrics designed for board-level consumption. Updated quarterly. Covers risk posture, top active threats, compliance status, and year-over-year trend data.

## Writing Guidelines

Executive briefings follow specific conventions to build trust with leadership over time. Lead with impact and business context, not technical details. Quantify wherever possible (accounts affected, dollars at risk, time to resolution). Use "higher quality" and "lower quality" language rather than "low risk" and "high risk" when describing account tiers. State what you know, what you don't know, and what you're doing to close the gap. Avoid hedging language that undermines confidence (not "we think we might have" but "we identified" or "we are investigating"). Close with clear asks if executive action is needed.
